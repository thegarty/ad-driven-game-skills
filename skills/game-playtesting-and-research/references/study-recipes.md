# Study recipes and interpretation

## Evidence levels

Source-inspected means a rule or interface was read; source-tested means a stated
behavior was actually exercised by a recorded check. Simulated means results
depend on a model or synthetic assumptions. Measured live means the production
population and collection window are known. A moderated playtest is an observed
research session: report its participants, build and assistance, and do not
relabel it as production retention. An attractive mockup or expert heuristic
supports a hypothesis, not a measured player outcome.

These are original research workflows, not universal sample-size or success-rate
rules. Select a method and decision threshold for the actual uncertainty.

## Match the method to the claim

| Question | Useful method | Observe | Claim limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can a new player understand the first choice? | Uncoached task followed by neutral questions | First action, errors, assistance and explanation | Does not establish long-term interest |
| Does a control or screen block particular players? | Targeted sessions using relevant devices/access methods | Task completion and recoverability | Generalisation requires the represented conditions |
| Why do people abandon a progression gate? | Journey observation plus stage-specific telemetry | Available choices, misunderstood rules, failure context | Interviews alone do not estimate prevalence |
| Is a new loop worth repeating? | Play followed by a genuine optional continuation | Voluntary replay, strategy change, stated reason | A short session cannot establish D7 retention |
| Does the reason to return survive absence? | Diary/return sessions with a stated interval | Remembered goal, return trigger, completion experience | Compensation and reminders can influence return |
| Does the intervention improve outcomes? | Appropriately designed controlled comparison | Predefined outcome and guardrails | Need comparable populations, exposure and sufficient maturity |

## Moderated session recipe

1. Confirm the build, fresh/returning save, test device and recording choice.
   Explain that the game is being tested, not the participant.
2. Give one goal in the participant's language without naming the required
   control. Keep developer explanations out of the task.
3. Observe silently at first. Ask neutral questions such as “What are you trying
   to do?” or “What do you expect now?” when they help the question being tested.
4. Use a predeclared assistance ladder: repeat the goal, ask what information is
   missing, provide a small hint, then demonstrate only if necessary. Record
   the point and level of help.
5. After the outcome, ask for the participant's explanation before explaining
   the designer's intention. Distinguish recall from recognition prompted by a
   list of possible answers.
6. Offer the next task or a real stopping point. Record whether another run is
   chosen voluntarily and why. Do not describe continuing as the desired answer.

Think-aloud can change concentration and timing. Use it to understand decisions,
but do not compare its elapsed times directly with silent ordinary play. For
precise control timing, observe a silent task and ask retrospective questions.

## Worked case: “players do not understand the upgrade”

Synthetic observations: one player opens the upgrade panel, reads the damage
number and chooses it; another looks for a way to stop fast enemies, sees no
counter description and repeats the same damage purchase. Both complete the
tutorial. A third player understands the counter but cannot distinguish the
enemy in a busy wave.

The evidence supports at least two hypotheses: weak counter explanation and
poor threat readability. It does not support a single conclusion that the
upgrade price is too high. Make the counter's purpose visible at the choice and
improve the threat cue, then test those changes separately where feasible.
Preserve whether a tester was coached; tutorial completion is a weak proxy for
understanding here.

## Worked case: a longer session

Synthetic sessions last longer after a new rewards screen. Break the duration
into combat, choice, loading, ads and navigation. Observation finds repeated
scrolling to locate Continue, while voluntary second-run choice is unchanged.
The supported result is added navigation time. Test the layout and exit path;
do not celebrate session-duration growth as engagement.

## Recruitment, coding and reporting

Recruit for the design question rather than only using team members or existing
fans. Keep prior genre experience, device constraints and relevant access needs
in the sampling plan without requesting unnecessary personal history. Compensate
time according to the study agreement, not positive feedback or continued play.
If sessions involve children, use the project's appropriate recruitment,
consent/assent and safeguarding process before contact.

Use observation codes with operational definitions: “assistance required” needs
the exact help; “confused” needs observable behavior; “successful” needs a stated
task outcome. Have a second reviewer code a subset when ambiguous interpretation
could change the decision. Disagreement is a reason to inspect evidence, not
average incompatible labels.

Report counts with denominators and conditions, anonymised examples, exclusions
and uncertainty. A participant quote can explain an experience; it cannot prove
frequency or causality. Keep raw recordings/private notes outside public case
studies. A clear findings report lists what changed the team's mind and what
remains unresolved.
